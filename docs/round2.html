The minor axis provides an accurate estimate of axon diameter only when the axon has a nearly cylindrical shape and is sectioned orthogonally. In reality, many axons exhibit elliptical cross-sections, causing the minor axis to underestimate the true diameter and the major axis to overestimate it, particularly when the axon is not cut perpendicular to its longitudinal axis. Measuring both axes does not fully resolve this issue. A reliable determination of the true axonal diameter ultimately requires three-dimensional reconstruction. Nevertheless, at the population level, diameter estimates derived from two-dimensional sections can be surprisingly accurate. As shown in a recent preprint (https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.03.25.714137v1.full.pdf), the circular-equivalent diameter appears to be the best proxy for the underlying three-dimensional diameter, as illustrated in Figure S2.

regarding the graphical/statistical analysis of g ratio plots: the method above is sub-optimal, but provides a good opportunity to balance heteroscedasticity in the repeated measures g ratio data. A simpler approach is to plot axon diameters vs fiber diameters for each animal. in the absence of statistical evidence for a non-linear correlation (i.e. Spearman's rho >> 0.7), the slope of the regression is the g ratio. Accordingly, there is no need to compute the g ratio from individual fibers, which introduces additional errors of measurement.
